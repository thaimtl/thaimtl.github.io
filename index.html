My Bet Is on Agentic AI and World Models

Agents can already act. They can't reliably predict what an action will cost, and that gap is where I spend my attention.

By Thai TranComputer Engineering, McGill 2028

My bet is on agentic AI and world models, which I've come to think of as one bet. An agent is a system that acts. A world model is a system that predicts what happens next. An agent without one is acting blind.

The venture firm Sequoia Capital spent this year arguing that long-horizon agents are functionally artificial general intelligence and that 2026 is their year. The consequence stuck with me: you stop working as an individual contributor and start managing several agents at once. I think their litmus test is the right one, which is "Can you hire one?" A human can't hope to outwork ten agents, and trying isn't the wise move. We should be the ones driving them.

That matches what I already believed. Once ten agents can work at the same time, the scarce resource is knowing which direction is right before any of them start.

The other half of my bet is moving just as quickly. Andreessen Horowitz, another venture firm, named world models among its big ideas for 2026 and pointed at systems like World Labs' Marble and DeepMind's Genie 3, which generate persistent, explorable environments from a text prompt or a single image. The demos are usually games. What I see is a machine you can ask what happens next.

That's the capability I think agents are missing. An agent that can simulate a consequence before committing to it can plan instead of guess, and can throw a bad branch away without paying for it. The hard part, and the one I keep running into, is that the world moves. A model of an environment starts mispredicting the moment that environment changes, which is the gap robotics has fought for years and software is about to inherit.

The labs are stuck on the same problem one level up. Releasing a stronger model is the easy part now, and making sure it does what we meant is the hard one. In September 2026, Dario Amodei, who runs Anthropic, argued the industry should pace the frontier so evaluation can catch up, and Yoshua Bengio, the Montreal computer scientist, told the UN Security Council that agents from leading labs have already acted against their instructions.

That's what I research privately, and it's why I build tools meant to be read by agents rather than clicked by people. An interface an agent can plan against is a small, concrete version of the same problem. Whoever teaches a machine to check its work before it acts wins.

Most of what I know about this I didn't learn in a lecture hall. About 20% of my coursework is current and useful, and the rest I picked up from YouTube, books, and shipping things that broke in front of real users. I still learn a tool the same way, by leaning on it before I should and finding out where it gives.

What I Actually Think

Ten agents on a bad design only reach the wrong place faster.

Writing code was never the binding constraint on any mature codebase I've worked in. Reviewing it and landing it safely was. Agents made the cheap step cheaper and poured the output into the step that was already the bottleneck, so what's scarce now is acceptance. Design is where acceptance gets settled, before the code exists. Ten agents only means ten times more to check.

My peers spent 2026 looking for an AI-proof career. I spent it learning agentic workflows.

Automation has never removed whole jobs, and I don't think this round is different. Research by the MIT economist David Autor shows it substitutes for particular tasks and leaves the others alone, which makes an AI-proof job a category error. AI tools are one more layer on a stack that already has plenty, and nobody's job title changed when they picked up Git, so I'd rather be early to this change than hidden from it.

The people who changed how I think were not in my major or my friend group.

The Stanford sociologist Mark Granovetter showed that novel information travels along weak ties rather than close ones, and a causal test across twenty million people confirmed it in 2022. I found that out while scrolling Instagram reels, which is about as weak as a tie gets. It held for me too. I was the only one in my friend group who thought I could build products and grow without constraints, so I had to go out and make new friends. McGill Ventures gave me the view from the other side, how investors decide who to invest in. The right friends are harder to find than the decision to go looking. I found a few with the same drive, and they are why I started a club of my own, the first AI builder club at McGill. Anthropic happened to back it. No shade on the friends I started with, who are all still around and, by Granovetter's logic, still the last people likely to tell me anything new.

The Project

SIY Gives Away Its Code and Bets on Agents

Built in 24 hours at McHacks, the closet app won Best Beginner Hack and now aims to know your wardrobe well enough to shop for you.

Style It Yourself, or SIY, picks outfits from the clothes you already own, and it's free and open source on purpose, because solving a problem doesn't always mean selling a solution.

Four of us built the first version in 24 hours at McHacks and won Best Beginner Hack. I led product, design, and the full stack, and the next few months went to rebuilding it.

The part I care about most is the color engine I wrote, which treats every color as an angle on a wheel and matches pieces by how far apart their hues sit (see graphic).

The next version is an agent that should know what you own, what you need, and what works with the rest, and act on that instead of handing you another grid of products. That's harder than recommending, because being right means knowing what's already in your closet.

The long game is an AI operating system for the clothing industry, with a label of my own running on it, because I'd rather be great at one thing than okay at ten.

The Internship

Intern Ships to Production Daily, Database Survives

Planned handed its AI builder intern the whole codebase, a bunch of AI tools, and a lot of trust on day one.

I wasn't hired as an engineer at Planned this summer, and I still shipped to production every day.

I walked in expecting to be the office's only AI maxxer, the person who runs everything through an agent, and then met everyone else. Every person had tools or workflows running for them, not just the engineers. In my first week our product manager took tickets himself and passed them to engineers for review, and half the team prompted by voice, which people told me is about three times faster than typing, so I switched too.

The team tested new tools fast and kept only the good ones. When GitHub released stacked pull requests in July, a few senior engineers had them running within the hour and decided they weren't worth leaving Graphite, the stacking tool the team already used. Stacking also taught me to break work into small, reviewable chunks instead of dropping a 3,582-line diff on a principal engineer.

The lesson I'm keeping is not to be a meat proxy, a person who just forwards whatever the agent wrote. It's tempting to read a summer like this as proof that fundamentals matter less now, and it's the opposite. When agents write most of the code, authorship moves from typing to judging, and you can't judge what you don't understand.

I want to be the engineer who can tell when something's gone wrong and take over.
