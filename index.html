My Bet Is on Agentic AI and World Models

Agents can already act. They cannot reliably predict what an action will cost, and that gap is where I spend my attention.

By Thai TranComputer Engineering, McGill 2028

My bet is on agentic AI and world models, which I have come to think of as one bet. An agent is a system that acts. A world model is a system that predicts what happens next. An agent without one is acting blind.

Sequoia spent this year arguing that long-horizon agents are functionally AGI and that 2026 is their year. The consequence stuck with me: you stop working as an individual contributor and start managing several agents at once. I think their litmus test is the right one, which is "Can you hire one". A human cannot hope to outwork ten agents, and trying is not the wise move. We should be the ones driving them.

That matches what I already believed. Once ten agents can work at the same time, the scarce resource is knowing which direction is right before any of them start.

The other half of my bet is moving just as quickly. a16z named world models among its big ideas for 2026 and pointed at systems like World Labs' Marble and DeepMind's Genie 3, which generate persistent, explorable environments from a text prompt or a single image. The demos are usually games. What I see is a machine you can ask what happens next.

That is the capability I think agents are missing. An agent that can simulate a consequence before committing to it can plan instead of guess, and can throw a bad branch away without paying for it. The hard part, and the one I keep running into, is that the world moves. A model of an environment starts mispredicting the moment that environment changes, which is the gap robotics has fought for years and software is about to inherit.

That is what I research privately, and it is why I build tools meant to be read by agents rather than clicked by people. An interface an agent can plan against is a small, concrete version of the same problem. Whoever teaches a machine to check its work before it acts wins.

Most of what I know about this I did not learn in a lecture hall. About 20% of my coursework is current and useful, and the rest I picked up from YouTube, books, and shipping things that broke in front of real users. I still learn a tool the same way, by leaning on it before I should and finding out where it gives.

What I Actually Think

Ten agents on a bad design only reach the wrong place faster.

Writing code was never the binding constraint on any mature codebase I have worked in. Reviewing it and landing it safely was. Agents made the cheap step cheaper and poured the output into the step that was already the bottleneck, so what is scarce now is acceptance. Design is where acceptance gets settled, before the code exists. Ten agents only means ten times more to check.

My peers spent 2026 looking for an AI-proof career. I spent it learning agentic workflows.

Automation has never removed whole jobs, and I do not think this round is different. Autor's task-based work shows it substitutes for particular tasks and leaves the others alone, which makes an AI-proof job a category error. AI tools are one more layer on a stack that already has plenty, and I would rather be early to that change than hidden from it.

The people who changed how I think were not in my major or my friend group.

Granovetter showed that novel information travels along weak ties rather than close ones, and a causal test across twenty million people confirmed it in 2022. I found that out while scrolling Instagram reels, which is about as weak as a tie gets. It held for me too. I was the only one in my friend group who thought I could build products and grow without constraints, so I had to go out and make new friends. McGill Ventures gave me the view from the other side, how investors decide who to invest in. The right friends are harder to find than the decision to go looking. I found a few with the same drive, and they are why I started a club of my own, the first AI builder club at McGill. Anthropic happened to back it. No shade on the friends I started with, who are all still around and, by Granovetter's logic, still the last people likely to tell me anything new.

The Project

SIY

An agentic ecommerce platform, and the thing I spend my nights on.

I started SIY as a closet that tells you what to wear out of clothes you already own. It is turning into something larger. The part I care about most is the recommendation side, which reasons about which pieces actually go together, working from a real wardrobe instead of pushing inventory at you.

Agentic ecommerce, the way I mean it, is an agent that understands what you already own, what you genuinely need, and what would work with the rest, and that can act on that understanding rather than hand you another grid of products.

That is a harder problem than a recommender, because being right requires knowing what is already in the closet.

The Internship

When Your User Is an Agent

Summer 2026. AI Builder Intern at Planned.

I spent the summer building internal tooling whose primary user was an AI agent rather than a person. That shift raises the bar on what counts as a good interface.

An agent arrives cold. There is no product tour, no colleague to ask, and no intuition about what I meant by a field name. Everything has to be legible from the schema alone, which turns out to be a demanding standard.

Working out that difference was the most useful thing I learned all year. The specifics stay inside the company.
