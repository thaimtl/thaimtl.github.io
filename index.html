Thai Tran

That’s me with my back to you, and my grandfather across the table.

Every afternoon in Vietnam, he played me at Chinese chess. I never learned to read the characters on the pieces, so I learned each one by the way it moves.

Now I study computer engineering at McGill, and I build the same way. Claude Code helps me write most of my code, but I know what every piece does.

Keep scrolling and I’ll tell you the rest between moves.

Origin

When my grandparents moved to my uncle’s, my dad took my grandfather’s seat. Nobody ever coached me, and in 9th grade I won my school’s inter-campus tournament anyway, the only 9th grader in it, against 12th graders.

The moves you’re watching aren’t mine, though. They’re real opening theory, the Central Cannon against the Screen Horses.

Rules

Learn the pieces like I did. Pick one and watch it move.

  • General

    Moves one point up, down, left or right and never leaves the palace.

    Red’s character means marshal. Black’s means general.

  • Advisor

    Moves one point along the palace diagonals and never leaves the palace.

    Red’s character means an official. Black’s means a scholar.

  • Elephant

    Moves exactly two points diagonally, can’t cross the river, and is blocked by a piece in between.

    Red’s character means minister and Black’s means elephant, and in Chinese the two words sound the same.

  • Horse

    Moves one point straight, then one diagonally outward, and is blocked by a piece on the point it steps through.

    Both characters mean horse.

  • Chariot

    Moves any distance in a straight line, with nothing in the way.

    Both characters mean chariot.

  • Cannon

    Moves like a chariot, and captures only by jumping exactly one piece.

    Red’s character is written with fire. Black’s is written with stone.

  • Soldier

    Moves one point forward, and after crossing the river it can also step sideways, never back.

    Red’s character means soldier. Black’s means foot soldier.

How to win

You win by checkmate. Attack the general so that nothing can save it, no escape, no block and no capture, and the game is over. A player with no legal move at all also loses, and the two generals may never face each other down an open line.

Silent Voice

My most recent hackathon. Cal Hacks 13.0, San Francisco, October 2026.

  • 2ndoverall, out of 650+ projects
  • 1stin Cartesia’s voice AI challenge
  • 36 hto build it

Four of us from McGill built a way to talk without making a sound, for people who lost their voice box to a laryngectomy, usually after throat cancer. You mouth the words, and the person on the other end of the call hears them in your own voice. In our demo, Austin called a judge standing in front of our table and only mouthed his side.

I was the only computer engineering major on the team, so the pads on his face were mine, four pairs reading the muscles underneath, and it turns out all those circuits classes were good for something. I also took the Claude side, which reads the conversation to tell look-alike words apart, and played PM for everything else, from shaping the vision to getting the pitch right.

Plan Your Degree

Open source. Shipped fall 2026.

  • 1,000+users in its first 5 days
  • 944programs
  • 143pull requests merged in 29 days

My own problem at McGill was smaller, but it kept coming back. The suggested curriculum for my program was so outdated that I stopped following it after first year, so every semester I had the catalogue, the registration system, and my own notes open across a dozen tabs to answer the same questions. What can I take next term? Do I have enough credits to graduate on time? What do I need before I can take that class everyone raves about?

So I built Plan Your Degree to answer them for me, then realized it was too useful to keep to myself and made it free for every McGill student. Drop in your unofficial transcript and it shows what you can take next and lays out every term until you graduate, with a warning for a missing prerequisite, a term a course isn’t offered, or a credit overload.

Under it, a crawler reads all 9,088 course pages and every program page in the catalogue and turns their plain-English requirements into trees the planner can check. Where only an advisor can judge a rule, it says so and lets you track it yourself. There’s no account and no backend, so your transcript never leaves your browser.

Planned

AI builder intern, April to August 2026.

A few friends and I spent the summer interning at Planned, a Montréal event planning startup I’d never heard of. It turned out to be super cracked, and I loved it there.

I worked under the AI product manager and shipped 50+ agent tools the engineers built on, plus the skills and docs that let non-technical operators run workflows in Claude without writing code.

The engineers, the product people, and the operations people all came from unusual backgrounds, none of them from big corporate tech, and every one of them was smart, fast, and AI native.

Working with them raised my bar for what good work looks like, and showed me I don’t need a big-title company to do meaningful, interesting work.

Club

  • 150+hackers

I was hunting for sponsors when I found Anthropic’s campus builder program. I applied, and in January 2026 I started the Claude Builder Club at McGill.

We get members free Claude Pro and teach them to use it properly, safely, and responsibly. I have non-technical friends who didn’t know what Git was a month earlier and now ship their own websites in an hour. Professors have also invited me into their engineering and management classes to run workshops.
